01 / Research focus

Research Question

Career decisions can involve both a need for practical information and a need for emotional support.

Does the effect of AI career advice style on users’ emotional responses and trust differ between users with Thinking–Feeling preference?

Target users: XJTLU students with career planning needs.

AI advice styleT/F preferenceEmotional responsesTrust
Experimental platform

A Wizard-of-Oz platform for Career Buddy

The AI we are evaluating is Career Buddy, but holding its reply style constant in a live conversation proved difficult — so for the experiment itself we built a purpose-made platform.

THE VARIABLE WE HAD TO CONTROL

Empathetic vs. analytical replies

A key manipulated variable in our design is whether the AI's reply is empathetic or analytical (thinking-type). When chatting with the live Career Buddy, the exact wording and tone of each reply are hard to keep consistent across participants and across the two conditions.

WHAT WE BUILT

A curated experiment platform

To control this variable, we built a dedicated experiment platform. It is pre-loaded with replies drafted by Career Buddy that our team reviewed and selected — a fixed set of empathetic replies and analytical (thinking-type) replies that we trigger on demand.

WHY IT MATTERS

Clean, comparable conditions

For each prompt and advice style, participants see the same wording. This makes the two styles reproducible and limits variation from generating a fresh reply for each conversation.

HOW IT RELATES TO BUDDY

Buddy writes, we select, the platform delivers

Career Buddy remains the source of the advice content; the platform is the controlled delivery layer used only during the experiment, so that the empathetic / analytical distinction stays clean and reproducible.

In short: Career Buddy is the agent being evaluated and the author of the reply drafts; the experiment platform is the controlled interface that presents a fixed, team-selected empathetic or analytical reply, so that AI reply style can be manipulated as a clean experimental variable.

Related Work

After an unsuccessful interview, a student may need help deciding what to do next and reassurance about the setback. Lee and Hahn examined how people perceive chatbots that offer informational or emotional support [1]. In a career-coaching trial with 82 participants, Ebner and colleagues found a greater reduction in negative affect over three weeks among those assigned to chatbot coaching than among those on a waiting list [2]. That comparison leaves open which features of a reply shape a student’s immediate emotional response.

Wording and source can each matter. In two health-advice experiments, Liu and Sundar varied how a chatbot expressed sympathy, cognitive empathy, or affective empathy [3]. Yin and colleagues found that people felt more heard after reading AI-generated responses, although attributing a response to AI reduced that benefit [4]. Trust raises a separate question. In forecasting experiments, people lost confidence more readily after seeing an algorithm make an error than after seeing a human make the same error [5]. Together, these findings motivate measuring perceived empathy, emotional response, and trust separately in Career Buddy.

Hough and Ogilvie studied cognitive style in strategic decision-making [6]. Whether Thinking–Feeling preference also shapes responses to career advice remains open in these studies. By presenting both reply styles to each student, we can compare how much emotion and trust change across styles within each preference group, then test whether those differences vary between groups.

02 / Study design

Experimental Design

A 2 × 2 mixed factorial design: T/F preference is a measured between-subjects factor, and AI advice style is a within-subjects factor. All 23 participants experienced both styles.

MANIPULATED CONDITION

AI advice style

A: analytical advice focused on practical information and reasoning. B: empathetic advice that acknowledges users’ feelings while offering career guidance.

MEASURED GROUPING VARIABLE

T/F preference

Thinking (T) or Feeling (F) decision-making preference, based on the MBTI Thinking–Feeling framework and explored with our pre-study questionnaire. This questionnaire is not the official MBTI assessment.

PRIMARY OUTCOMES

Emotional responses & trust

Participants rated their emotions at baseline and after each style (GEW), then rated their trust in each style (TIAS). We tested whether the difference between styles in positive emotion, negative emotion, or trust varied by T/F group.

SECONDARY MEASURE

Perceived empathy

Participants rated each style on the 10-item Perceived Empathy of Technology Scale (PETS). We compared overall perceived empathy across styles and explored the emotional responsiveness and understanding and trust subscales.

04 / Experiment plan

Experiment Demonstration

We recruited by convenience sampling. Each of the 23 participants experienced both advice styles and evaluated each one.

Design2 × 2 mixed factorial; every participant evaluates both AI styles.
SamplingConvenience sample of 23 experimental participants (10 T, 13 F).
CounterbalancingThe system randomly assigns the order of styles (AB or BA) to control for order effects.
Data collectionPre-study questionnaire, GEW, TIAS, PETS, and brief interview comments.

Complete Experimental Procedure

Step Content
Step 0 Participants read and sign the informed consent form.
Step 1: Scenario & Baseline Participants read a standard interview failure scenario to induce career anxiety. After reading, they immediately complete GEW 1 as baseline emotional data.
Step 2: Interaction 1 Participants input the first preset script (expressing anxiety about the interview failure). Based on random assignment, the system presents the first AI response style (Analytical or Empathetic). After reading, they complete GEW 2/3 to measure emotional changes.
Step 3: Interaction 2 Participants input the second preset script (expressing future job-hunting plans). The system presents the second AI response style (different from Step 2). After reading, they complete GEW 2/3 to measure emotional changes.
Step 4: Comprehensive Evaluation After experiencing both styles, participants complete a post-study questionnaire. They evaluate their trust (TIAS) and perceived empathy (PETS) separately for the "first style" and the "second style".
Step 5: Interview A brief semi-structured interview is conducted to ask about participants' subjective preferences and reasons. Finally, we express our gratitude (Debriefing).

Experimental results

Data analysis

Data were analysed in IBM SPSS Statistics using separate 2 × 2 mixed ANOVAs for positive emotion, negative emotion, trust (TIAS), and perceived empathy (PETS total score). Advice style (empathetic vs analytical) was the within-subjects factor, and T/F preference was the between-subjects factor. All 23 participants (10 Thinking, 13 Feeling) evaluated both styles. The analyses tested the effects of style, preference group, and their interaction; statistical significance was assessed at α = .05.

From submissions to condition scores

The pre-study questionnaire supplied screening information. The analysis used the recorded T/F group for each participant, including those without a reliably matched pre-study score. A submission entered under an incorrect ID was excluded and the later correctly labelled submission was retained. A make-up session was included.

Each participant contributed one row. Post-session GEW and PETS/TIAS responses were assigned to the style actually experienced using the recorded session order. This aligned scores even when participants experienced analytical advice first.

How the outcomes were scored

For each post-session Geneva Emotion Wheel (GEW), we averaged the ten positive and ten negative emotion ratings separately. Explicit N/A responses were scored as zero. The baseline GEW described the starting state. The style comparison used the two post-session ratings.

For trust (TIAS), the first five items were reverse-scored as 8 minus the response before averaging all 12 items (1–7). Perceived empathy was the mean of the ten PETS items (0–100). All 23 participants contributed paired scores for each outcome.

Why a mixed ANOVA?

Each person rated both styles but belonged to only one preference group. For each outcome, the style-by-preference interaction tested whether the change from analytical to empathetic advice differed between T and F participants. The style main effect estimated the average difference between conditions, giving the two groups equal weight.

Assumptions and uncertainty

With two styles, sphericity holds automatically. Levene’s tests on within-person style differences gave p values from .320 to .758 across the four outcomes. Shapiro–Wilk tests suggested departures from normality for positive emotion in T (p = .031) and trust in F (p = .017). These results warrant caution given the small groups.

We report unadjusted p values for the four models, partial η² for effect size, and 95% confidence intervals for selected contrasts. Testing multiple outcomes increases the chance of a false positive.

Interview analysis

We reviewed the 18 available post-session interviews (11 Feeling, 7 Thinking). We read the Chinese responses for three questions: which reply participants preferred, what would feel comforting or reliable after an unsuccessful interview, and what they wanted a future assistant to do. We grouped comments about feeling comforted, perceiving empathy and judging reliability. We compared accounts within and across the recorded T/F groups, keeping comments about an experienced reply separate from wishes for a future assistant. We used the recorded session order to interpret numbered replies where available and left ambiguous style references unresolved. Each interview counted once, including where a translation or repeated passage appeared. The excerpts below are English translations labelled with anonymous participant codes.

Brief interpretation

Empathetic advice received higher PETS total scores than analytical advice (M = 73.39 vs 61.94). The main effect of advice style was statistically significant, F(1, 21) = 8.49, p = .008, partial η² = .288. The model-estimated difference, weighting the two preference groups equally, was 10.99 points, 95% CI [3.14, 18.83], indicating higher perceived empathy for empathetic advice.

Average trust scores were almost identical (both rounded to 4.93), and the style effect was not significant (p = .835). Neither positive emotion (p = .570) nor negative emotion (p = .244) showed a significant style effect. The interaction tests in Table 1 were also not significant (all p > .05), so the observed differences between the T and F response patterns remain inconclusive. These results do not establish that the styles or preference groups are equivalent.

Findings

Answer to the research question

There is insufficient evidence to support the hypothesis that the effect of AI career advice style on users’ emotional responses and trust differs between users with Thinking and Feeling preferences.

Perceived empathy

The mixed ANOVA results suggest that there is a statistically significant difference in perceived empathy (PETS total score) between the empathetic and analytical advice conditions, with empathetic advice receiving higher scores, F(1, 21) = 8.49, p = .008, partial η² = .288.

Emotion, trust and T/F preference

However, no statistically significant differences between the two advice conditions were detected in positive emotion, negative emotion, or trust. No significant main effects of T/F preference or interactions between advice style and T/F preference were found for any of the four outcomes.

How precise is the trust comparison?

The estimated shift in trust from analytical to empathetic advice was 0.48 points smaller in T than in F. The 95% confidence interval for this interaction ranged from −1.02 to 0.07 points on the seven-point scale. It includes zero and also allows for a potentially meaningful group difference.

How did Thinking and Feeling participants experience the two advice styles?

In the interviews, participants sometimes gave different answers when asked which reply felt better and which they would rely on after an unsuccessful interview.

Perceived empathy and emotional response

P07 experienced empathetic advice first and analytical advice second. The first reply felt better, yet P07 preferred the second for its specific solution.

“I actually felt a bit better with the first one … I still need AI to give me a concrete solution.” P07 · Thinking

This distinction helps interpret the two measures. PETS asked how empathetic the assistant seemed and showed higher ratings for empathetic advice. GEW measured participants’ own emotions and showed no significant style effect for either positive or negative emotion.

What made a reply feel trustworthy?

P12 linked trust to the assistant’s ability to reason through the problem. P16 likewise distinguished the reply that offered comfort from the one they considered more reliable.

“I’d definitely trust the first response more … I think AI is better suited to working things through logically and giving practical advice.” P12 · Feeling
“If it’s about making me feel a little better, I’d say the first one. But for reliability, I’d say the second.” P16 · Thinking

These accounts describe individual judgments. Across all 23 participants, mean TIAS trust scores rounded to 4.93 under both styles.

Did the accounts follow a T/F pattern?

Within the Feeling group, P04 valued the warmth of the empathetic reply, while P12 valued the reasoning in the advice. Within the Thinking group, P07 described feeling better after empathetic advice, while P02 preferred to turn to friends for comfort.

“I’d lean towards the second one, because it’s like my mum. It feels warm.” P04 · Feeling, empathetic reply second
“If I need comfort, I can go to my friends.” P02 · Thinking

The interviews illustrate variation within both groups. In the mixed ANOVAs, the style-by-preference interactions for positive emotion (p = .072), negative emotion (p = .163) and trust (p = .082) provided insufficient evidence that advice style affected the groups differently.
